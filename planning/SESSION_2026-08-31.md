## 2. Part A — the public site

### What was asked

Put the adaptive-search result on `docs/index.html`, upload the graph PNG, and
add the Qualcomm benchmark in detail.

### Decision A1 — regenerate the chart rather than paste it

`simulation/results/found_vs_time.png` is drawn on white. `docs/index.html` is
committed dark. Dropping the file in reads as a hole punched in the page.

Three options were considered:

1. Paste it as-is, framed as a light "paper figure" pinned to a dark board.
2. Rebuild the chart as inline SVG in the page.
3. **Re-render the same data in the site palette.** ← chosen

Option 1 fights the entire visual system for no gain. Option 2 would mean the
chart's shape lives in two places — the Python that made the figure and the SVG
in the page — and the two would drift.

Option 3 keeps one source of numbers. `simulation/make_site_chart.py` reads
`results/results.json`, the file `run.py` already wrote. It cannot re-run the
experiment and does not try to. Re-run `run.py`, then this, and both figures
move together.

### Decision A2 — the baseline line colour was chosen by measurement, not eye

The obvious pairing was brand cyan `#22A7BD` for adaptive against `--dim`
`#6B7F87` for the lawnmower baseline. Run through a colour-separation check
against the `#070B0D` page ground, that pair fails:

| pair | normal vision | deuteranopia |
|---|---:|---:|
| `#22A7BD` vs `#6B7F87` | 9.8 ΔE | 6.2 ΔE |
| `#22A7BD` vs **`#5A6E76`** | **16.9 ΔE** | **14.8 ΔE** |

Below about 15 ΔE for normal vision, a reader has to consult the legend to tell
two lines apart — which defeats the chart. `#5A6E76` was chosen because it
passes *and* stays darker than the cyan, so the baseline still recedes and the
subject still leads. Reversing that (a lighter grey) would have passed the
numbers while inverting the visual hierarchy.

Colour is not carrying identity alone regardless: the baseline is dashed, the
subject is solid and heavier, and there is a legend.

### Decision A3 — group-header rows instead of `rowspan`

The results table pairs two planners under each prior quality. `rowspan="2"` on
the prior label vertically centres it across two rows of unequal height, and
with only one of the pair highlighted it reads as a rendering fault. Replaced
with a full-width group header row (`GOOD PRIOR · CORRELATION +0.65`) above each
pair. Fewer columns, clearer grouping, and it survives the phone breakpoint.

### Bug A4 — a class-name collision that inflated every highlighted row

Highlighted table cells were given `class="n hero"`. `.hero` is the **page
header's** class:

```css
.hero{padding:72px 0 56px; ...}
```

A `<td class="hero">` inherits that padding. Every highlighted row rendered
**145 px tall instead of 44 px**. It looked like a screenshot artefact and I
nearly wrote it off as one; measuring the row heights in the browser found it in
one call. Renamed to `.win`.

The lesson generalises: this stylesheet uses very short class names — `.n`,
`.a`, `.b`, `.h`, `.f`, `.t`, `.v`, `.k`. Most are compound-scoped
(`.step .n`, `.vz-c.a .v`) and safe, but any new bare class needs checking
against the file first. I renamed my own `.cap` to `.figcap` for exactly this
reason before it bit.

### Bug A5 — a broken image on the live site

`docs/index.html` referenced `img/disaster-detections.png`. **That file does not
exist anywhere in the repo.** The "Trained on disaster scenes" block has been
rendering a broken-image icon to every visitor.

Replaced with the site's own styled placeholder rather than deleting the block,
because the surrounding claim is true and worth keeping — the model *is* trained
on C2A disaster imagery. **The figure still needs generating**: same weights,
conf 0.18, imgsz 960, over a handful of C2A images.

### Verification

Rendered at 390 / 768 / 1440 px in headless Chromium. No horizontal body
overflow at any width; wide tables and the chart scroll inside their own boxes
as designed. Every figure on the page was recomputed from
`simulation/results/results.json` and the four Qualcomm JSON records and matched

## 3. Part B — triaging the SIH plan PDF

### The core judgement

The PDF is a good plan sized for about three weeks. This morning it had five
days. **Attempting all fourteen items in § 20 produces fourteen half-finished
things**, and half-finished is worth less on stage than four things that work.

Its own § 1 table already marks **eleven of thirteen capabilities BUILT**. Most
of what it asks for is strengthening, not construction.

### The two genuine gaps it found

1. **No random-search baseline.** `planners.py` has lawnmower and adaptive;
   § 3.2 wants three. This one is worth doing and is cheap — see D3 below for
   the subtlety.
2. **No hazard/risk penalty in the utility function.** § 3.4 specifies
   `U = αP + βIG − γC − δR`. Ours is `(belief + staleness) / cost` — no `R`.
   Which means **the search planner and the rescue-priority scorer currently
   disagree about whether hazards matter.** That is a real inconsistency, not a
   missing feature.

### Decision B1 — do not rebuild the utility for an explicit information-gain term

§ 3.4 wants a separate `βIG` term. Our staleness term already *is* an
information-gain proxy: a cell nobody has looked at recently carries more
uncertainty, so it scores higher, and the Bayesian miss-update means one pass is
never proof of absence. That is a defensible answer to a judge and it is
already benchmarked over 100 seeds.

Adding a formal IG term means re-deriving the utility and re-running the whole
sweep — a research refactor. **Cheaper and more honest to name the existing term
properly in the docs than to rebuild and re-validate under deadline.**

### Recommended cuts, and why

| PDF item | Call | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Navigation simulation (A*/RRT) | **Cut** | The adaptive planner already chooses targets. A path planner between them adds nothing a judge will notice. |
| Webcam + Pi hardware-in-loop | **Cut** | The Qualcomm NPU measurements are stronger evidence of edge capability than a webcam rig. |
| Borrowed-drone demonstration | **Cut** | No time, and § 21 forbids claiming autonomous flight without it anyway. |
| Expected-information-gain term | **Cut** | See B1. |
| Seven hazard classes | **Cut to 3** | The PDF says this itself: *"Do not claim seven strong classes if only three have reliable evidence."* |

### One point taken on the chin

§ 21: *"Do not spend excessive time polishing the website while core
experiments remain incomplete."* Part A of this session was website work. The
frame-rate study is what the rest of the day went to, and it is the right
ratio going forward.

## 4. Part C — the frame-rate study

**The main work of the day, and it changed what the project can claim.**

### The question

`backend/data/detections.json` was produced by running the model over **every
frame** of a 24 FPS clip. The device does not do that. One inference on the
Dragonwing RB3 Gen 2 takes 209.5 ms, so it manages **4.8 frames per second** —
a little under one frame in five.

> Does the confirmed survivor count survive the drop to the frame rate the
> hardware actually delivers?

Everything on the dashboard is downstream of tracking, and tracking is the part
that should care: between two processed frames the aircraft has travelled five
times as far, so boxes jump five times as far — and ByteTrack matches boxes by
**how little they moved**.

### Decision C1 — re-run the tracker rather than decimate the JSON

The cheap version of this test is to filter `detections.json` down to every 5th
frame and re-count. It would have taken ten minutes.

**It would also have been worthless.** Those records already carry track IDs
assigned by ByteTrack *at 24 FPS*. Decimating them tests the persistence filter
while assuming the tracker succeeded — and the tracker is the thing under
suspicion.

So the model and the tracker were re-run from the video, twice, at both rates.
Cost: the weights and clip staged into the container, ultralytics installed, and
about six minutes of CPU inference per full pass. Worth it — the cheap version
would have returned a clean bill of health.

### Decision C2 — skipping frames is the right model of a slow device

The device is not handed a slower video. It is handed the same flight and
manages fewer looks at it. Dropping four frames in five is exactly what a
processor that cannot keep up does. Re-encoding the clip to 4.8 FPS would have
changed the footage; skipping changes only the sampling.

### Decision C3 — compare people, not track IDs

ByteTrack numbers tracks in order of appearance. Skip four frames in five and
the order changes, so **id 7 in one run and id 7 in the other are different
people**. Comparing IDs would have produced a confident, meaningless answer.

Confirmed survivors are matched **by where and when they were** — two tracks
pair if they appear on the same original frame within 60 px, greedily, closest
first. This decision is what made § C7 possible.

### Result 1 — the count does not survive

| | full rate | device rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| effective FPS | 24.0 | 4.8 |
| frames processed | 312 | 63 |
| raw detections | 7,059 | 557 |
| detections per frame | 22.6 | **8.8** |
| track ids | 328 | 94 |
| persistence threshold | 60 frames | 12 frames |
| **confirmed survivors** | **21** | **10** |

### C4 — the mechanism, from one anomalous number

**Detections per frame fell from 22.6 to 8.8 on the *same frames*.** A detector
is stateless — frame 0 is frame 0 whether or not you also looked at frame 1. So
that number cannot be a detector effect, and it is the thread that unravels the
whole thing.

`model.track()` does not return raw detections. It returns **what the tracker
accepted**. ByteTrack's second association stage keeps low-confidence boxes only
when they match an existing track — and our operating point, conf 0.18 chosen
for recall, lives on exactly those boxes.

One cause explains both halves of the result: five times the frame gap → five
times the pixel motion → IoU against the previous box collapses → association
fails → **tracks die and take their low-confidence detections with them.**

### Result 2 — two parameters appear to fix it

| rate | tracker | det/frame | ids | **confirmed** |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| full | bytetrack-default | 22.6 | 328 | **21** |
| device | bytetrack-default | 8.8 | 94 | 10 |
| device | bytetrack-wide | 10.9 | 76 | **20** |
| device | botsort-gmc | 17.3 | 78 | 47 |

`bytetrack-wide` is the shipped tracker with `match_thresh` 0.8 → 0.95 (the
IoU-distance gate, too tight when boxes barely overlap) and `track_buffer`
30 → 60 frames.

**20 against a full-rate 21.** At this point the study looked finished, and the
write-up would have said "two parameters fix it."

### Decision C5 — verify identity before believing the count

The count matching is not evidence that the *people* match. Twenty unrelated
tracks would print the same number. So a third script pairs every device-rate
survivor onto the full-rate reference by position, and — deliberately — **lets
two candidates claim the same reference person**, because a one-to-one matcher
would discard the duplicate as "unmatched" and hide the exact failure being
looked for.

### Result 3 — the fix was a false positive

| device-rate tracker | reports | real people found | missed | duplicates | worst split |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| bytetrack-default | 10 | 9 / 21 | 12 | 1 | 2 |
| **bytetrack-wide** | **20** | **13 / 21** | **8** | **5** | 2 |
| **botsort-gmc** | 47 | **20 / 21** | **1** | 17 | 5 |

> `bytetrack-wide` reaches 20 by **missing 8 real survivors and double-counting
> 5**. The two errors partly cancel into a plausible number.

That is the most dangerous failure mode this system has — a wrong number that
survives inspection because it is close to the expected one. **Had the study
stopped at Result 2, the repo would now contain a config change described as a
fix, and the dashboard would report a survivor count assembled from two
cancelling errors.**

**BoT-SORT with global motion compensation is the only configuration that
actually sees the people**: 20 of 21, missing one. Its problem is the opposite
and it is *visible* rather than hidden — it splits identities, once into five,
and reports 47.

### C6 — what this means

The two problems are separable, and only one is solved:

| | at 4.8 FPS |
|---|---|
| **Finding** survivors | Solved — BoT-SORT + GMC holds 20 of 21 |
| **Counting** them once | **Not solved** — every config either misses people or splits them |

`backend/tracks.py` already named this limitation in prose:

> *"an ID switch, where one person picks up a second ID after an occlusion.
> Both halves persist past the threshold, so both are confirmed and the survivor
> count is one too many. That is a tracker problem."*

It is now measured rather than anticipated, and it is worse at device rate.

### C7 — the demo is not affected

The demo replays a pre-computed `detections.json` produced at the clip's full
24 FPS, exactly as `CLAUDE.md` requires. **Nothing on stage depends on
device-rate tracking.** What this study bounds is the *claim*, not the
demonstration.

### C8 — a caveat that must travel with these numbers

The full-rate rerun gives **328 ids / 21 confirmed** where the shipped
`detections.json` documents **333 / 23**. The difference is ultralytics 8.4.135
here against 8.4.129 when the file was produced. Close enough to validate the
harness; not identical. **Quote the shipped file's numbers for the demo and
these for this study, and never mix them.**
