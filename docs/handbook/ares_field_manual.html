01 — Architecture

Four stages, each solving what the last one leaves behind

Detection has no memory. Tracking has no location. Localization has no ordering. Every stage exists because the previous one produces something not yet usable.

03 — Defence

Judge Q&A

If you do not know, say so and say what you would do to find out. A confident wrong answer is the only kind that loses the room.

Open with this, before they ask

The sixty-second framing

ARES detects survivors from drone imagery, tracks them so we don't double-count, puts them on a map, and ranks them for rescue order.

Three things you should know first. We don't own a drone, so the footage is a public UAV dataset — the detections are our own model's real output over it. The demo replays a pre-computed file rather than inferring live; the model already ran. And some of what we describe isn't built — autonomous navigation and SLAM are architecture, not code, and the dashboard doesn't pretend otherwise.

Each of those, discovered by a judge, is a credibility hit. Volunteered, each is evidence of judgment.

The model

Your confidence threshold is 0.18. Isn't the default 0.5?

It is, and we changed it deliberately. In search and rescue the costs are wildly asymmetric — a false alarm costs a rescuer thirty seconds of walking, and a missed survivor cannot be recovered.

We read the threshold off the precision-recall curve from our validation run. At 0.5, recall is 0.740. At 0.18, it's 0.831. That's ninety-one more people found per thousand present. The price is more false positives, and we handle those downstream with a persistence filter rather than by raising the threshold and losing people.

This is your strongest answer. Take it slowly.

That's epoch 44 of 100. Why did you stop?

The Colab session died. We validated the interim weights, they were good enough, and with twelve days left an integration-tested end-to-end system was worth more than the one to three points of mAP the remaining epochs would likely add. Model accuracy was never our bottleneck.

Answer honestly. This is a test of whether you'll spin.

Counting and tracking

333 track IDs but 23 survivors. Isn't the tracker broken?

No — that gap is the price of the 0.18 threshold, and it's the other half of that decision. At a recall-first threshold the detector reports anything person-shaped: a shadow, a bag, a patch of rubble. The tracker gives each one an ID.

We measured those short tracks — mean confidence 0.42 against 0.52 for long ones, and only 7% starting or ending at the frame edge. Low confidence, mid-frame, gone in a frame or two is flicker, not a person walking out of shot. So persistence is the right filter. Dropping the confidence threshold instead would have cost real survivors.

You had a tracking bug, didn't you?

A bad one. We were getting 350 IDs for about 23 people. We thought it was camera motion — measured it, 1.4 pixels per frame, not the cause. We thought it was duplicate boxes from failing NMS — measured it, 1.12 boxes per cluster, not that either.

It was input resolution. Detecting at 640 on a 1280-wide clip halved every frame, so a 24-pixel person reached the network at 12 pixels — below what the model resolves reliably. Detections flickered, and no tracker can hold an identity across boxes that keep vanishing. We swept 640, 960 and 1280 and took 960 on the measurements.

Two plausible hypotheses killed by measurement before the right one. That is what diagnosis looks like.

Does the persistence filter fix everything?

No. It removes flicker. It does not fix ID switches — where one person picks up a second ID after walking behind something. Both halves persist, so both get confirmed and the count is one too many. That needs a better tracker, not a longer threshold.

Do not oversell this. The known limitation is an asset, not a liability.

Localization

How do you get GPS from a camera?

Nadir projection with a known altitude. A camera pointing straight down from 20 metres with a 60-degree field of view sees 2 × 20 × tan(30°) — about 23 metres of ground across 1280 pixels. That's 1.8 centimetres per pixel. A pixel offset from the frame centre is a ground offset from the drone's position, and metres convert to degrees at 111,320 per degree of latitude, or that times the cosine of the latitude for longitude.

How accurate is it?

Good enough to put a pin on the right building. Not survey grade.

The error budget is dominated by assumed altitude and attitude — a 10% altitude error is a 10% scale error, terrain relief adds more, and a five-degree tilt off nadir shifts the footprint by nearly two metres. Realistically one to two metres of relative uncertainty. The flat-earth approximation contributes about a millimetre, so replacing it with a proper geodesic would be theatre.

Never overclaim this one.

Priority scoring

So on this clip the score is just detection confidence?

On this clip, yes, and the dashboard says so in those words. Hazards aren't measured — that term is dropped. And all 23 survivors are inside an 11-metre patch, so every one has the same 22 neighbours; a term with the same value in every row can't rank those rows, so it's dropped too and the remaining weight renormalises.

What's left is confidence. And ordering by confidence isn't meaningless here: at a 0.18 threshold a low-confidence box is about as likely to be a shadow as a person, so the queue is ordered by how likely each dispatch is to find someone. We verified the cluster term works when the data allows it — a synthetic scene with an isolated survivor and a pair 200 metres away scores 0.0, 0.25, 0.25.

They may well spot this. Have it ready.

Why is a survivor scored 0.76 sitting in the "high" band when the cut is 0.75?

Hysteresis, and it's stated on that panel. A band is a state that gets re-read, and the confidence term jitters frame to frame — so a track parked near a cut oscillates across it. Our log showed single tracks changing band five times in nine seconds, which reads as an unstable assessment when what's unstable is one bounding box's confidence.

There's a three-point deadband around each cut: climbing to critical needs 0.78, falling back to high needs below 0.72. It delays a band change; it never hides one.

Why is the hazard list empty?

Because the hazard classifier is Phase 2 and we haven't trained it yet. We could have put a fire on the map to make it look busier. Then the priority ranking would be responding to something we invented.

The scoring code treats a missing hazard layer as not measured and drops the term entirely, rather than scoring it zero — because "no hazard nearby" and "no hazard detector" are different claims.

On-device

It'll only manage about 1 FPS. Isn't that useless?

It sounds low next to video frame rates, and it's exactly what you'd expect from a 9-million-parameter attention model on a Cortex-A72 with no accelerator.

It's also enough. At 20 metres altitude the camera sees 23 metres of ground, so at search speed the aircraft takes about five seconds to cross its own footprint. Every patch of ground is visible in five or more consecutive frames even at 1 FPS. A missed detection in one frame is recovered in the next. Thirty FPS would give us thirty looks at the same ground, twenty-nine of them redundant, at thirty times the power budget. A search UAV doesn't need 30 FPS. It needs to not miss the ground.

This is the answer that wins the hardware round.

Hostile

This is just YOLO with a dashboard, isn't it?

The detector is off the shelf and we'd be lying if we said otherwise. What's ours is everything between a detector and a decision.

A raw detector gives you 7,081 boxes. Getting from there to "twenty-three people, here they are on a map, go to this one first" is de-duplication, evidence thresholding, coordinate projection and a ranking you can explain. Every one of those has a failure mode we hit and fixed. And using an off-the-shelf detector is the right call — training our own architecture would be worse and would have consumed the twelve days.

How do I know you didn't just draw those boxes?

Scrub the video. The boxes flicker in and out on marginal detections and the confidence values vary continuously — that's what a detector at a 0.18 threshold looks like. Hand-drawn boxes would be steadier and cleaner than this. The script that produced the file is in the repo, and you can re-run it.

What's your biggest weakness?

ID switches. If someone walks behind a structure and comes back out, they can pick up a second identity, and both halves clear our persistence threshold, so the count is one too many. We know it happens, we know persistence doesn't fix it, and the dashboard states the limitation rather than implying it's solved. The fix is a re-identification-capable tracker, which is more compute — a measured trade we haven't made yet.

Never say "we don't have any."

04 — Judgment

Decisions, and what they beat

The pattern running through all of them: choose the less impressive, more defensible option.

YOLOv12s

Ultralytics-native so tracking and export work without integration work. 9.3 M params — the size where edge deployment is plausible and small-object accuracy survives.

Beat: YOLOv8n/s, RT-DETR, Faster R-CNN

Pre-computed replay

The model already ran; this is what it produced. Live inference on stage adds a failure mode that proves nothing.

Beat: live inference during the demo

A JSON file as the contract

Decouples three people's code — Robin needs no PyTorch install to develop localization against real data.

Beat: a shared Python module, a database

A transparent weighted formula

A judge is asking how rescue order is decided. "A neural network decides" is a bad answer to that question.

Beat: a learned ranker, a decision tree

Drop a term, don't zero it

A zero claims a measurement nobody made. Renormalising redistributes rather than capping everyone below 1.

Beat: scoring 0.0, or leaving it flat

One reference frame for distance

The assumed track turns time separation into ground separation. At 5 m/s a three-second gap invents fifteen metres.

Beat: each track's own frame origin

An ordinal priority ramp

The old rainbow failed twice — no perceptual order, and red-green collapses for ~8% of men at ΔE 10.6.

Beat: green → amber → orange → red

Mission parameters, not telemetry

There is no aircraft. A battery bar would be six invented numbers dressed as instrument readings.

Beat: battery, GPS signal, link quality, weather

Persistence as a duration

2.5 s is 60 frames here and 3 on a Pi. Neither number is wrong; the unit is.

Beat: a hardcoded frame count

05 — Corrections

What went wrong, and how it was caught

A judge who believes you made no wrong turns believes you did not do the work. Three of these were confident wrong diagnoses killed by measurement.

B3

"It must be camera motion"

The drone was supposedly moving fast enough to break IoU association. Measured: 1.4 px/frame. Not the cause.

B4

"It must be duplicate boxes"

NMS supposedly failing, each person spawning several tracks. Measured: 1.12 boxes per cluster. Also wrong. The real cause was input resolution.

B6

"Attention will be worse on CPU"

Predicted YOLOv12's attention would be disproportionately penalised without GPU bandwidth. Measured CPU ratio 2.18× against GPU 3.14× — relatively better than predicted.

B7

min_frames = 3 — a unit error

A constant written where the justification was frame-rate dependent. Three frames is 0.125 s at 24 fps and 2 s at 1.5 fps. Now expressed as a duration.

B9

"Pick the densest sequence"

Maximum density made de-duplication read as a broken system rather than a working filter. Moderate density lets a judge count by eye and confirm your number.

B10

Estimating spread from eight visible rows

Guessed 7 × 11 m from a screenshot. Measured across all 23: 27 × 29 m. Then measured properly at a fixed reference frame: 11.38 m. Two wrong numbers in a row from skipping the measurement twice.

B11

The moving-origin cluster bug

Track 1409 — the highest-confidence detection in the clip at 0.802 — ranked 21st of 23, because its last sighting was three seconds after everyone else's. The ranking was ordering on when people were last seen.

B12

.venv-ml in the git push

The gitignore listed .venv/, which does not match .venv-ml/. 182 MB pushed, rejected by GitHub's 100 MB file limit. Gitignore patterns are literal.
